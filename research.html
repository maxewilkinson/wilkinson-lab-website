Research

We discover and study reverse transcriptases using biochemistry, microbiology, and structural biology.

Why study reverse transcriptases?

A primary goal of the lab is to discover new biology. We want to uncover new, nucleic-acid based phenomena. But where should we look?

One way to address this question is to look at how known phenomena evolved. It turns out that a remarkable amount of interesting biology evolved from an ancient, prokaryotic mobile genetic element — the group II intron. This is most famous as a catalytic RNA that excises itself from mRNAs. It is less well-known that the group II intron often encodes an ORF with reverse transcriptase and endonuclease activities. These activities are collectively used to allow the excised intron to cut a new piece of DNA, and reverse transcribe itself into a new location - thus creating a new intron. Even though this sounds like a niche (but fascinating) piece of prokaryotic biology, each of these activities has been co-opted by evolution over time to give rise to:

  • pre-mRNA splicing by the spliceosome. RNA-catalysed splicing is transferred to host snRNAs, the RT domain is transferred to host Prp8
  • Telomere extension. The RT domain binds a pseudoknotted ncRNA to template telomeric repeat synthesis
  • Retrotransposons like LINE-1. These are simply the eukaryotic descendents of group II introns. They have lost self splicing but preserve everything else.
  • Myriad prokaryotic systems. Phage-defence systems, diversity-generating retroelements, CRISPR systems that can acquire spacers from RNA...
It's even plausible that the group II intron was the precursor to retroviruses like HIV that have such devasating impacts on human health.

One could argue that >95% of the human genome derives from descendents of the group II intron - whether it's the non-coding introns, or the LINEs, SINEs, and ERVs that fill all the gaps.

So if so much interesting biology evolved from the group II intron, and if we want to find more interesting biology, then one strategy is to look at all of the mysterious, uncharacterised descendents of the group II intron that are scattered throughout domains of life.

This is the current strategy of the Wilkinson lab. We do this by bioinformatics, microbiology, genetics, biochemistry, and structural biology.

Reverse transcription for bacterial immunity

Just like us, bacteria are under constant threat of infection by viruses (called phages). We've found that many bacteria are armed with sophisticated viral defence systems powered by reverse transcription. While the mechanisms of these defences are largely mysterious, we recently discovered how one such system works. We found that upon phage infection, a bacterial reverse transcriptase synthesises a toxic repetitive gene from an RNA template. The single infected cell stops growing and thereby saves the entire bacterial population from the virus.

We are now investigating the mechanisms of other reverse transcriptase-based defence systems, to uncover what we expect to be a remarkable diversity of mechanisms that showcase the creativity of evolution.

Jumping genes: mechanisms of retrotransposons

Eukaryotic genomes, including our own, are littered with 'jumping genes' called retrotransposons. In fact, nearly 40% of the human genome is derived from a single type, the LINE-1 element. To understand these abundant elements, which are associated with genomic diseases and cancers, we study the widespread R2 element as a model system. R2 inserts itself specifically into ribosomal DNA arrays.

We have previously solved the structure of the R2 protein to reveal how it finds its target DNA and have shown that R2 can be used to direct therapeutic transgene insertion in human cells. We are now investigating the fundamental mechanisms of how R2 moves and how its expression is helped by and/or interferes with ribosome biogenesis. Our goal is to understand how these molecular parasites survive in a genome, and how we can adapt their activity for medicine.
